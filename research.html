Housing Like Irish Weather?

Undergraduate thesis, UCD School of Economics, April 2026. Supervised by Prof. Eoghan O'Neill.

Volatility regimes and asymmetric transmission in the Irish housing market. The question: does demand feed through to housing differently in calm and stressed times?

Identification. Regimes are identified from the demand block (modified domestic demand, rent-to-income, unemployment and CPI) rather than from house prices, so the thing that defines a regime is kept separate from the outcomes it is meant to explain.

The 𝔍𝔄 prior. A hierarchical Student-t shrinkage prior for Markov-switching VARs. Shrinkage intensity τA, lag decay φ and tail thickness νA are estimated jointly with the coefficients, and it nests the Minnesota prior as a limiting case (νA → ∞, φ → 1).

The inference. A differentiable Hamilton filter inside the No-U-Turn Sampler marginalises the regime sequence at every leapfrog step, so regime uncertainty flows through every stage instead of being frozen as a generated regressor.

What I found

Ninety-eight quarters of Irish data. The asset block, not house prices themselves, is where the regime story lives.

28 quarters

One high-volatility episode in the asset block, 2008Q2 to 2015Q1. Five times longer than the matching demand episode, consistent with a financial accelerator keeping stress alive long after the shock.

About 2×

The credit block's response to the shadow rate is roughly twice as large in the high-volatility regime, but stress there is short-lived: a two to three quarter window for policy.

5.09×

Construction cost volatility amplifies by this factor in the high-volatility regime, while quantities don't follow. Supply is held back by non-price constraints.

36.6%

The long-run probability of the high-volatility regime in the asset block. House prices themselves show only modest, imprecise regime separation.

The policy point: macroprudential tools calibrated on single-regime estimates, or triggered by price thresholds, risk being miscalibrated in exactly the periods where calibration matters most.

The prior

𝔍𝔄 prior

A Student-t prior on every VAR coefficient. As ν → ∞ and φ → 1 it approaches the Minnesota prior.

Ar,jb​∼iidT1​(νA​, 0, σr,j​)

In words

Each coefficient is drawn independently from a fat-tailed bell curve centred on zero. Most get squeezed hard towards zero, but the heavy tails leave room for the few large ones the data really call for.

VAR coefficient

One coefficient in block b: how strongly regressor r feeds into equation j. There are lots of them, so each one needs a sensible prior.

Independent draws

Each coefficient gets its own independent draw, so the squeeze is applied coefficient by coefficient.

Student-t distribution

A bell curve with fatter tails. Most coefficients get squeezed towards zero, but a few big ones can survive when the data insist.

Tail thickness (degrees of freedom)

Small means heavy tails, large means the prior turns into a Gaussian. It is learned from the data rather than fixed. Drag the slider below to see it.

Centre

The prior is centred on zero, so coefficients are pulled towards zero unless the data push back.

Prior scale of one coefficient

How tightly this particular coefficient is squeezed. It comes from the global shrinkage and the lag it belongs to.

Shrinkage by lag

Older lags get squeezed harder.

σr,j​=τA​ℓ(r)−φ
where ℓ(r)=⌈r/nb​⌉

In words

The prior scale shrinks with lag number: the global tightness divided by the lag raised to the decay power. The further back a lag, the tighter the squeeze.

Prior scale of one coefficient

How tightly this particular coefficient is squeezed. It comes from the global shrinkage and the lag it belongs to.

Global shrinkage

The overall tightness of the squeeze. Smaller means stronger shrinkage and simpler dynamics. It is learned from the data.

Lag number

Which lag regressor r belongs to: 1 is last quarter, 2 is the quarter before that, and so on.

Lag decay

How fast the squeeze tightens on older lags. A bigger value shrinks distant lags harder than recent ones.

Regressor to lag

Turns a regressor’s position into a lag number. With n_b variables in the block, the first n_b regressors are lag 1, the next n_b are lag 2, and so on.

What the data learns

The shrinkage settings get their own priors, so tightness is estimated rather than guessed.

τA​∼HN(1)
φ∼Exp(1)
νA​∼Γ(2,0.1)

In words

None of the three settings is picked by hand. Each gets its own prior and is estimated jointly with the coefficients, so the data decide how tight the squeeze is.

Prior on global shrinkage

Half-normal: keeps the shrinkage scale positive, with most of its weight at modest values.

Prior on lag decay

Exponential: keeps the decay rate non-negative and lets the data decide how steeply older lags are penalised.

Prior on tail thickness

Gamma: keeps the degrees of freedom positive and wide enough to allow anything from very heavy tails to almost Gaussian.

Shock covariance

Scales and correlations are separated, so each gets its own prior.

Ωb​=Db​Rb​Db​

In words

The covariance of the shocks is split into standard deviations on the outside and a correlation matrix in the middle, which makes each part easy to give a sensible prior.

Shock covariance

The covariance of the shocks hitting the equations in block b.

Shock scales

A diagonal matrix holding each equation’s shock standard deviation, each with a half-normal prior.

Shock correlations

How the shocks move together across equations. Its Cholesky factor gets an LKJ prior with η = 1, which is uniform over all valid correlation matrices.

The regimes

Regime transitions

A softmax with a persistence boost keeps regimes sticky without hard-coding it.

pij​=∑m=1K​exp(ζim​+κδim​)exp(ζij​+κδij​)​

In words

Each row of scores is turned into probabilities with a softmax. The κ term gives staying put a head start, and the data decide how much persistence is real.

Transition probability

The chance of being in regime j next quarter given regime i this quarter. The diagonal entries are the persistence.

Latent score

An unconstrained number for each transition, with a standard normal prior. The sampler can move it freely, and the softmax turns it into a probability.

Persistence boost

δ is 1 on the diagonal (stay put) and 0 elsewhere, so κ adds weight to staying in the same regime. It is only a nudge: the data decide how persistent regimes really are.

Normalising sum

Sums over all regimes so each row of P adds up to 1. That lets the sampler work with free parameters instead of constrained probabilities.

How sticky are the regimes?

One number for how quickly the chain forgets its starting regime.

gap(P)=1−∣λ2​∣
gap(P)=1−∣p00​+p11​−1∣

In words

Near 0 means regimes last a long time, near 1 means the chain flips around quickly. In the thesis, the asset block comes out at 0.195 (very persistent) and property prices at about 0.63 (fast turnover).

Spectral gap

How fast the regime chain forgets where it started. Near 0 means persistent, slow-switching regimes. Near 1 means rapid turnover.

Second eigenvalue

A two-regime transition matrix has eigenvalues 1 and λ₂. The closer |λ₂| is to 1, the longer regimes last.

Two-regime shortcut

With two regimes, λ₂ works out to the two stay-probabilities added together, minus one.
